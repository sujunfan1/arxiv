## 2. Optimistic Hedge in General-Sum Games: Fundamental Limits and Regret Bounds via Temporal Approximation

原始摘要：Optimistic Hedge, a multiplicative weight update with one step of extrapolation, is one of the most natural regret-minimizing dynamics for games, and characterizing its regret has been a longstanding open question. Daskalakis, Fishelson and Golowich proved that if all $m$ players in a finite general-sum game follow Optimistic Hedge with a fixed learning rate, each player's regret is polylogarithmic in the horizon $T$. Subsequently, Ha showed that an appropriate choice of learning rate makes each player's regret bounded independently of $T$. This bound, however, is non-constructive and its dependence on the number of actions $n$ is not explicit, leaving open whether Optimistic Hedge can achieve a constant regret bound which is polynomial in the size of the game.
  We show that the answer hinges on the number of players. In two-player games, there is a learning rate for which Optimistic Hedge guarantees $O(n\log n)$ regret independent of the horizon. In three-player games, however, no such bound is possible: for any learning rate up to an absolute constant and for sufficiently large horizon $T$, there is a game with $n$ actions per player in which some player's regret is $2^{Ω(n)}$, and more generally, for every $m\ge3$ we construct $m$-player games in which some player's regret is $2^{Ω(mn)}$; for learning rates above $2^{-Ω(mn)}$, that player's regret even grows linearly in $T$.
  Our upper bound is based on a temporal approximation framework for the analysis of Optimistic Hedge. Starting from the variance-based RVU bound of Daskalakis et al., we approximate the losses over time-localized windows by well-behaved functions, whose total variation is a small fraction of their cumulative variance. In $m$-player games, the same framework gives a significantly simplified proof of the recent $O(\sqrt{m}\log n\log T)$ regret bound due to Ha.

原始摘要：Animals and humans often do not maximize; they act relative to goals (aspiration levels) that are frequently not yet met. How can a goal that is not (yet) realized organize choice? We examine this idea in a minimal formal setting: Risk-sensitive Satisficing (RS), a bandit learning rule that values each action by its share of past trials multiplied by its surplus or shortfall relative to an aspiration level. 原始摘要：LLM-guided evolutionary search uses AI agents to improve programs for mathematical and computational problems by iteratively refining solutions and combining high-scoring methods. However, the process is often slow because the LLM generates a long textual chain of thought for each round and must repeat this generation many times to maintain population diversity. We present SwarmEvolve, a framework in which hundreds of low-cost agents per round work in swarms, reason in latent space without decoding to text, and share information by passing KV caches. Each swarm studies the problem from a different angle. A novelty filter keeps the most distinct agent ideas by directly measuring the distances between the agents' hidden state vectors. Crossover of ideas happens naturally in latent space, as a summarizer agent receives the combined KV caches of all swarm members. On 21 problems in mathematical discovery and scientific computing, SwarmEvolve gets the highest average score and needs a median of only 3 to 8 minutes to reach the best scores that other state-of-the-art frameworks reach in 60 minutes. On several problems, SwarmEvolve with Qwen3 8B improves on the solutions that AlphaEvolve first found with frontier LLMs. Our work shows that agent swarms that communicate in latent space offer a fast, low-cost approach to program search for scientific problems.
